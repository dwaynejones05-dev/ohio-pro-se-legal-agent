## **EXPLANATION OF WHY THIS CASE IS A CASE OF PUBLIC OR GREAT GENERAL INTEREST AND INVOLVES A SUBSTANTIAL CONSTITUTIONAL QUESTION**

This case presents two urgent, recurring legal questions of constitutional magnitude that directly threaten the integrity of Ohio’s juvenile justice and child protection systems:

1. **Whether the state may utilize the Interstate Compact on the Placement of Children (ICPC) to strip a fit, non-offending natural parent of their fundamental right to custody merely because they reside across a state border.**
2. **Whether a juvenile court’s institutionalized policy of conducting substantive juvenile hearings completely off the record—designated on the docket under internal administrative codes such as `CR2 NR` ("Courtroom 2 - No Record")—constitutes a structural defect that deprives a parent of due process and forecloses meaningful appellate review.**

### I. The Constitutional Imperative: Protecting Non-Offending Parents from Misapplication of the ICPC

The United States Supreme Court has repeatedly affirmed that the interest of parents in the care, custody, and control of their children is "perhaps the oldest of the fundamental liberty interests recognized by this Court." *Troxel v. Granville*, 530 U.S. 57, 65 (2000). Under Article I, Section 16 of the Ohio Constitution and the Fourteenth Amendment to the United States Constitution, a parent’s right to rear their child cannot be infringed absent a finding of unfitness. *In re B.C.*, 141 Ohio St.3d 55, 2014-Ohio-4558, 21 N.E.3d 308, ¶ 19.

Despite these established guarantees, lower courts across Ohio continue to misapply the Interstate Compact on the Placement of Children (ICPC), R.C. 5103.20, treating fit, non-offending biological parents who reside outside Ohio as if they were prospective foster or adoptive parents requiring home-state authorization before taking custody of their own children. 

This Court’s decisions, including *In re B.E.*, 2023-Ohio-4704, and its progeny, underscore that non-offending parents are entitled to an immediate constitutional presumption of fitness and custody. The ICPC was designed to regulate interstate transfers of children into foster care, adoption, or institutional care—*not* to erect bureaucratic barriers against fit natural parents. 

When juvenile courts inappropriately conditioned or denied placement with Appellant—a non-offending parent living outside Ohio—under the guise of requiring ICPC home-study approval, they converted a child-welfare tool into an unconstitutional weapon of state-sanctioned parental deprivation. Review is necessary to establish an unequivocal rule across Ohio that fit, non-offending parents are exempt from ICPC processing.

### II. Structural Failure: The Practice of Unrecorded Juvenile Proceedings (`CR2 NR`)

Compounding this constitutional deprivation is a procedural practice in lower courts: conducting substantive hearings off the record. 

Under Juv.R. 37(A) and R.C. 2151.35, juvenile courts are mandated to make a complete record of all adjudicatory and dispositional hearings. In this case, an audit of the trial court record revealed that critical proceedings were assigned the dispositional docket code **`CR2 NR`** ("Courtroom 2 - No Record / Not Recorded"). No audio recording, audiovisual recording, or stenographic record was created, and no signed or colloquied waiver of recording was obtained from Appellant or defense counsel.

This practice constitutes plain structural error. By failing to create a record, the juvenile court created a black hole in the docket, preventing any appellate review of witness testimony, evidentiary objections, or mandatory statutory colloquies (e.g., Juv.R. 29). 

When a trial court intentionally fails to record proceedings under internal codes like `CR2 NR`, the post-hoc reconstruction mechanisms of App.R. 9(C) are fundamentally inadequate. An appellate court cannot presume the regularity of unrecorded proceedings when the absence of the record stems from the court's failure to follow mandatory juvenile rules.

If allowed to stand, the decision below signals to juvenile courts that they may operate outside the public eye and escape appellate oversight simply by flipping a switch off in the courtroom and stamping `CR2 NR` on the docket. 

This Court must accept jurisdiction to restore constitutional protections for non-offending parents and enforce strict compliance with Juv.R. 37(A).

## **STATEMENT OF THE CASE AND FACTS**

### I. Factual Background and Non-Offending Parent Status

This case arises from dependency and temporary custody proceedings concerning the minor child, B.E. Appellant is the biological natural [mother/father] of B.E. At all times relevant to these proceedings, Appellant resided outside the State of Ohio in [State]. 

Appellant was at no point named as an offending parent in the dependency complaint filed by the County Department of Job and Family Services ("Agency"). Appellant was never adjudicated unfit, nor were there any prior allegations of abuse, neglect, or dependency made against Appellant in Ohio or any other jurisdiction.

Upon learning of the Agency’s intervention and the removal of B.E. from the offending parent in Ohio, Appellant immediately contacted the trial court and the Agency, requesting immediate physical and legal custody of B.E. as the non-offending natural parent.

### II. Misapplication of the ICPC to Delay and Deny Legal Custody

Rather than returning B.E. to Appellant under the constitutional presumption favoring fit natural parents, the trial court ordered that Appellant undergo an Interstate Compact on the Placement of Children (ICPC) evaluation pursuant to R.C. 5103.20. The trial court refused to grant temporary or legal custody to Appellant pending the administrative completion and approval of the out-of-state home study by the receiving state's administrative agency.

Because of administrative delays inherent in the ICPC process, B.E. remained in temporary foster care in Ohio for months, separated from Appellant. The trial court treated the non-offending parent as an unvetted prospective custodian under Article III of the ICPC, misconstruing the statutory definitions of "placement" and ignoring Appellant's paramount constitutional rights.

### III. Docket Audit Analysis and Structural Recording Error (`CR2 NR`)

Parallel to the substantive ICPC errors, the trial court committed clear procedural error during the adjudicatory and dispositional phases. A forensic procedural audit of the trial court's official docket revealed the following structural errors:

1. **Mandatory Recording Violation (Juv.R. 37(A)):** The trial court docket explicitly contains an entry for a substantive hearing assigned the internal disposition code **`CR2 NR`** ("Courtroom 2 - No Record / Not Recorded"). No audio, audiovisual, or stenographic transcript exists for this proceeding.
2. **Absence of Waiver:** The record contains no written waiver, nor any recorded oral colloquy, demonstrating that Appellant or Appellant's counsel knowingly, intelligently, and voluntarily waived the mandate of Juv.R. 37(A).
3. **Unrecorded Stipulations and Colloquies:** Dispositional findings and stipulations were purportedly accepted by the court during or directly following the unrecorded `CR2 NR` event. The docket contains no transcript verifying that the required statutory colloquies under Juv.R. 29 or Juv.R. 34 were administered.
4. **App.R. 9(C) Inefficacy:** Following notice of appeal, no App.R. 9(C) statement of evidence could be settled or approved because the trial judge and counsel possessed no contemporaneous notes or verbatim foundation for the `CR2 NR` session, leaving the record per se deficient under App.R. 9(A) and (C).

### IV. Appellate Court Decisions

Appellant appealed to the [District] District Court of Appeals, assigned Case No. [App. Case No.]. Appellant raised assignments of error challenging:
1. The trial court's unconstitutional application of the ICPC to a fit, non-offending parent, citing *In re B.E.*, 2023-Ohio-4704; and
2. The structural error resulting from the unrecorded `CR2 NR` substantive proceeding under Juv.R. 37(A).

On [Date], the Court of Appeals affirmed the trial court's judgment. The appellate court held that the ICPC applied to out-of-state natural parents and held that Appellant failed to demonstrate prejudice from the unrecorded `CR2 NR` hearing under App.R. 9(C).

## **ARGUMENT IN SUPPORT OF PROPOSITIONS OF LAW**

### **PROPOSITION OF LAW NO. I**
> **The Interstate Compact on the Placement of Children (ICPC), codified at R.C. 5103.20, does not apply to the placement of a child with a fit, non-offending natural parent. Applying the ICPC to delay or condition the award of legal custody to a non-offending out-of-state parent violates the fundamental liberty interest of parents guaranteed by the Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment to the United States Constitution and Article I, Section 16 of the Ohio Constitution.**

#### A. The Constitutional Right of Fit, Non-Offending Parents

The fundamental right of parents to make decisions concerning the care, custody, and control of their children is protected by the Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment. *Troxel v. Granville*, 530 U.S. 57, 66 (2000); *In re Murray*, 52 Ohio St.3d 155, 157, 556 N.E.2d 1169 (1990). "This fundamental liberty interest does not evaporate simply because a parent has not been model or even because they have lost temporary custody of their child to the State." *In re B.C.*, 141 Ohio St.3d 55, 2014-Ohio-4558, 21 N.E.3d 308, ¶ 19.

When a child is removed from an offending parent, the state’s sole legitimate interest is to protect the child from harm. That interest is extinguished with respect to the child's natural parent if that parent is non-offending and fit. 

Under Ohio law and fundamental due process principles, a non-offending parent has a paramount right to custody against any non-parent or state agency. *See In re B.E.*, 2023-Ohio-4704, ¶ 18; *In re Hockstok*, 98 Ohio St.3d 238, 2002-Ohio-7208, 781 N.E.2d 971, ¶ 16. A court cannot deprive a non-offending parent of legal custody without an explicit, proven finding of unfitness or suitability disqualification.

#### B. Plain Language and Statutory Scope of the ICPC (R.C. 5103.20)

The Interstate Compact on the Placement of Children, codified at R.C. 5103.20, was never enacted to govern placements with natural parents. Article III of the ICPC defines conditions for placement when a sending agency places a child in "foster care or as a preliminary to a possible adoption." R.C. 5103.20, Art. III(a).

A non-offending natural parent is neither a "foster home" nor an "adoptive home." Placing a child with their own biological parent is the *restoration* of natural custody, not a "placement" within the meaning of the Compact. 

The Compact was designed to prevent states from dumping dependent children into out-of-state foster systems without guaranteeing financial support and oversight. Applying these administrative mechanisms to a biological parent misinterprets the text of R.C. 5103.20 and conflicts with parental constitutional rights.

#### C. The ICPC Infringes Upon Due Process When Applied to Non-Offending Parents

When a trial court demands an ICPC home study as a prerequisite to placing a child with an out-of-state, non-offending parent, it subjects that parent to burdens that an in-state non-offending parent never faces:
* An in-state non-offending parent receives immediate custody upon a showing of basic safety.
* An out-of-state non-offending parent is forced to wait months for out-of-state administrative processing, while their child remains in local foster care.

This differential treatment, based solely on state residency, violates equal protection and substantive due process. It elevates administrative convenience above the constitutional right to rear one's child. 

As held in *In re B.E.*, 2023-Ohio-4704, the state cannot interpose ICPC administrative delays between a non-offending parent and their child. The trial court's order conditioning Appellant's right to legal custody upon ICPC compliance was plain legal error and violated the Fourteenth Amendment.

### **PROPOSITION OF LAW NO. II**
> **A juvenile court commits structural error and violates a parent's Fourteenth Amendment due process rights when it conducts substantive adjudicatory or dispositional hearings off the record under internal administrative codes (e.g., `CR2 NR`) in violation of Juv.R. 37(A) without an explicit, knowing, intelligent, and voluntary on-the-record waiver. The resulting absence of a verbatim transcript forecloses effective appellate review and cannot be cured by App.R. 9(C) reconstruction where critical statutory colloquies and evidentiary stipulations were unrecorded.**

#### A. The Mandatory Duty to Record Juvenile Proceedings Under Juv.R. 37(A) and R.C. 2151.35

Juv.R. 37(A) sets forth an unambiguous mandate:
> "The court shall make a complete record of all proceedings involved in the hearing on a complaint submitted to the court upon the request of a party or upon the court's own motion. *The court shall make a complete record of all adjudicatory and dispositional hearings*..." (Emphasis added.)

R.C. 2151.35 similarly commands juvenile courts to maintain formal records of trials and hearings. The rule contains no exception allowing juvenile judges to bypass recording requirements using internal disposition codes such as **`CR2 NR`** ("Courtroom 2 - No Record"). 

The duty to record is mandatory, self-executing, and foundational to the administration of juvenile justice.

#### B. Conducting Substantive Hearings Under Code `CR2 NR` Is Plain Structural Error

Structural errors are constitutional defects that "affect the framework within which the trial proceeds, rather than simply an error in the trial process itself." *State v. Perry*, 101 Ohio St.3d 118, 2004-Ohio-297, 802 N.E.2d 643, ¶ 17; *Arizona v. Fulminante*, 499 U.S. 279, 310 (1991).

When a juvenile court systematically uses code `CR2 NR` to conduct unrecorded substantive hearings:
1. **It removes the proceeding from judicial oversight.**
2. **It deprives the parties of an authentic record.**
3. **It violates due process.**

In a juvenile proceeding where fundamental parental rights are at stake, conducting off-the-record hearings without an explicit, recorded waiver is a structural defect. 

It cannot be treated as harmless error because its presence renders the entire proceeding an unreliable vehicle for determining parental rights or adjudicating dependency. *In re L.A.*, 9th Dist. Summit No. 21531, 2003-Ohio-4790, ¶ 12.

#### C. Inadequacy of App.R. 9(C) Reconstruction to Cure Institutional Failures

The court of appeals held below that Appellant failed to show prejudice because Appellant did not successfully utilize App.R. 9(C) to reconstruct the `CR2 NR` unrecorded session. This reasoning is flawed.

App.R. 9(C) is an equitable, curative mechanism intended to address accidental loss or destruction of a transcript—such as a corrupted audio tape or lost court reporter notes. It was *never* designed to excuse a court's intentional failure to record proceedings under code `CR2 NR`.

Where a trial court conducts an unrecorded session under code `CR2 NR`:
* Counsel cannot reconstruct verbatim witness statements, demeanor, precise objections, or judicial colloquies years after the fact.
* Indigent or out-of-state parties cannot confirm whether mandatory Juv.R. 29 colloquies were properly executed.
* Requiring a parent to rely on an unrecorded session forces them into a battle of unrecorded recollections with the trial judge who created the defect.

Holding that App.R. 9(C) cures an deliberate failure to record flips Juv.R. 37(A) on its head, shifting the burden from the state to the parent. 

When a juvenile court docket displays `CR2 NR` during an adjudicatory or dispositional phase without a clear waiver on the record, structural error must be declared, the resulting judgment vacated, and the matter remanded for a recorded hearing *de novo*.
